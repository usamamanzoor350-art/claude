Your six-week sleep reset

The six weeks

Daytime

Talk with your doctor

Many women are never asked about their sleep. These questions can help you start the conversation.

Questions you might ask

  • Could hormone therapy be a good option for my hot flashes and sleep?
  • If hormones aren't right for me, are there non-hormonal medicines for hot flashes, such as fezolinetant?
  • Could my snoring, waking or daytime tiredness be sleep apnea? Should I have a sleep study?
  • Can you refer me to someone who offers CBT for insomnia (CBT-I)?
  • I wake up to use the bathroom most nights. Is there something we can look at?
  • Could any of my current medicines be affecting my sleep?

Mention these right away

  • Loud snoring, gasping or choking in your sleep, or someone noticing you stop breathing
  • Falling asleep without meaning to during the day
  • An uncomfortable urge to move your legs in the evening or at night
  • Feeling low, hopeless or anxious most days

Sleep apnea becomes more common after menopause and is often missed in women, partly because it can show up as insomnia or tiredness rather than snoring.

Daytime

Why this works

Every part of Softly to Sleep is built on techniques that have been studied with real people. Here is what is behind each one.

Waking in the night is the most common menopause sleep complaint

Between about 35% and 60% of women report sleep problems around menopause, and repeated night waking is the main one. In sleep-lab studies most hot flashes come with an awakening, yet a lot of night waking happens without a hot flash. That's why Softly to Sleep has separate tools for heat and for a busy mind.

The six-week reset: CBT for insomnia

CBT-I is the first treatment medical guidelines recommend for chronic insomnia. A 2025 review of 11 trials in women with menopausal insomnia found it improved sleep quality, including when it was delivered remotely. In one trial with women who had hot flashes, the benefit lasted at least six months. The reset uses gentle, self-guided steps from CBT-I. It is inspired by CBT-I but is not a replacement for therapy with a trained provider.

"I woke up hot": cooling imagery

In clinical hypnosis research, guided imagery of coolness (snow, cool water, a breeze) was part of programs that cut hot flashes substantially for many postmenopausal women, including programs women listened to on their own at home.

"My mind won't quiet": the word game

Picturing a slow series of unrelated, peaceful things is called serial diverse imagining, or cognitive shuffling. In a study of people kept awake by a busy mind, it eased pre-sleep arousal and helped them fall asleep more easily.

"I'm just awake": rest without trying

Trying hard to sleep keeps the brain alert. Letting go of the effort, sometimes called paradoxical intention, has been shown to reduce sleep effort and worry about sleep.

"Been awake a long while?": leaving the bed for a while

Getting up when you're restless, and returning when sleepy, is called stimulus control. It is one of the best-supported parts of CBT-I and helps the bed feel like a place for sleep again.

What we don't claim

Slow breathing is included to help you relax, but trials have not shown that it reduces hot flashes, so we don't promise that. Softly to Sleep does not diagnose or treat any condition.
